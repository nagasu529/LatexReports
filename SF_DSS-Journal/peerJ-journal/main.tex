\title{A Data-Driven Decision Support Architecture for Agricultural Systems: Integrating Cluster-Conditioned Explainable AI and Surrogate Rules}

\author[1]{Kitti Chiewchan}
\author[2]{Jumtip Seneerattanaprayul}
\affil[1]{Computer Education Program, Faculty of Education, Bansomdejchaopraya Rajabhat University, Bangkok, Thailand}

\corrauthor[1]{Kitti Chiewchan}{kitti.ch@bsru.ac.th}

\begin{abstract}
Allocating training programs to farmers with different backgrounds requires transparent methods that explain which farmer characteristics 
lead to better learning outcomes. We propose SAR (Segment--Attribute--Recommend), a systems architecture for targeted resource allocation. SAR combines three stages: 
(1) grouping farmers into segments based on their profiles, (2) identifying the key characteristics within each segment, and (3) converting these findings 
into simple IF--THEN decision rules for practical use.

We test SAR on agricultural training outcomes using data from $n=833$ farmers in Thailand's Smart Farmer program. 
The system identifies three farmer segments with different drivers of skill improvement: baseline experience,
 production management capability, and educational background. The extracted decision rules achieve 91.4\% 
 accuracy on held-out test data, 95.3\% $\pm$ 2.5\% cross-validated accuracy, and 93.3\% accuracy on the full dataset.

SAR offers a transparent, auditable framework for targeted resource allocation in agriculture and policy settings. 
Unlike black-box algorithms, SAR produces human-readable rules that non-technical administrators can apply without 
machine learning expertise.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Distributing training programs and resources across farmers with very different needs is a key challenge in agricultural development. Most national extension 
programs still use the same curriculum for all farmers, regardless of their skill level or background. This uniform approach often means that some farmers 
learn little, resources are wasted, and few new practices get adopted. To improve outcomes, programs need to match each farmer to the right type of 
training based on their skills, risks, and available resources. 

While machine learning models have improved predictions across many fields, using them for policy decisions creates one key requirement: the decisions 
must be explainable. Black-box models are not suitable for public sector use, where decision-makers need to understand, justify, and defend every 
recommendation. Moreover, good resource allocation is not just about predicting outcomes — it also requires knowing why certain farmer 
characteristics lead to different results across different groups.

Explainable AI (XAI), and in particular SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), offers a reliable way to measure how much each variable contributes to a model's 
prediction. However, current XAI work has two practical limitations. First, most studies stop at producing charts and graphs — they do not convert these 
explanations into clear rules that practitioners can follow. Second, most methods report a single importance ranking across all farmers, which hides the fact that 
different farmer groups respond to training in very different ways. A factor that strongly predicts skill gains for one group may have almost no effect for another.

To address these gaps, this study proposes SAR (Segment--Attribute--Recommend), a three-stage decision support system that makes explainable AI practical for 
real-world use. SAR works by: (1) grouping farmers into segments, (2) identifying the most important characteristics within each segment, and (3) turning those 
findings into simple IF--THEN rules. The main contribution is that these rules can be used directly by extension officers and policymakers — no machine learning 
knowledge required. This closes the gap between AI research and actual deployment in the field.


\section{Literature Review}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Machine Learning and Computer Vision in Agriculture}

Machine learning and computer vision are now widely used in modern agriculture
for tasks such as crop monitoring, disease detection, yield estimation, and
livestock identification. \citet{ghazal2024survey} found that image-based
methods remain the most common approach throughout the crop production cycle.
For cattle identification, CNN-based models tend to perform better than
classical machine learning when enough training data are available. Random
Forest and deep learning models have also been applied to nutrient prediction,
while classical methods remain competitive for plant disease detection,
suggesting that method choice should depend on the data at hand.

However, most of these studies focus on sensing and detection tasks. Few
connect their outputs to practical decisions that farmers or extension officers
can act on. This gap motivates the need for systems that go beyond prediction
and provide clear, usable guidance.

\subsection{Explainable AI for Agricultural Decision Support}

As machine learning models become more complex, explaining their decisions 
has become essential for building trust in agricultural systems 
\citep{arrieta2020,linardatos2021}. \citet{guidotti2019} group explanation 
methods into three types: feature attribution, surrogate modelling, and rule 
extraction — all of which are used in SAR.

SHAP is one of the most widely used tools for explaining tree-based models 
because it is based on a fair and consistent mathematical framework 
\citep{lundberg2017}. \citet{lundberg2020} showed that SHAP gives stable 
results across different model types, including Random Forest and XGBoost. 
In agriculture, SHAP has been applied to yield prediction, disease diagnosis, 
and risk assessment \citep{ghosal2018,ryo2022}.

However, most XAI studies in agriculture stop at describing what the model 
learned — they do not turn those insights into rules that farmers or 
extension officers can use \citep{linardatos2021,arrieta2020}. 
\citet{ryo2022} specifically call for frameworks that convert XAI outputs 
into practical decision tools. Applying SHAP separately within each farmer 
segment — rather than across all farmers at once — is also still largely 
unexplored. SAR addresses both of these gaps.

\subsection{Farmer Heterogeneity and Training Effectiveness}

Farmers vary in their skills, resources, and attitudes toward risk, which 
means they respond differently to the same training program 
\citep{feder1985,anderson2004}. Research shows that farmers who already 
have stronger skills tend to gain more from advanced training \citep{davis2012}. 
Despite this, most extension programs still offer the same content to all 
farmers, mainly because they lack the tools to do otherwise \citep{anderson2004}. 
SAR directly addresses this problem by using data to match each farmer to 
the most suitable training track.

\subsection{Large Language Models in Agricultural Intelligence}

Recent studies have explored how large language models (LLMs) can bring 
together scattered agricultural knowledge through fine-tuning and reasoning 
\citep{li2025llm}. However, most of these applications are still theoretical 
and have not been tested in real farming contexts. SAR takes a different 
approach — it is built on actual farmer data and produces decisions that 
extension officers can apply directly in the field.

\subsection{Research Gaps}

Five gaps motivate this study:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Explanations are not turned into usable rules.} Most XAI 
  studies produce visual insights but do not convert them into clear rules 
  that extension officers can follow.

  \item \textbf{No complete pipeline from data to decision.} Existing systems 
  rarely combine farmer segmentation, predictive modelling, and rule extraction 
  into a single, ready-to-use system.

  \item \textbf{SHAP is applied globally, not by segment.} Most studies 
  measure feature importance across all farmers at once, missing the 
  differences between farmer groups.

  \item \textbf{Attribution results are not linked to decision rules.} Few 
  studies connect feature importance findings to practical rules that 
  administrators can act on.

  \item \textbf{Non-technical users are overlooked.} Little research examines 
  how extension officers or policymakers — without machine learning knowledge — 
  actually use AI explanations in practice.
\end{enumerate}

SAR addresses gaps 1--3 by measuring feature importance separately within 
each farmer segment and converting those findings into simple IF--THEN rules 
for extension use.

\subsection{On Causal Interpretation and Policy Deployment}

All findings in this study show associations, not causal relationships. 
However, this does not make the results less useful for policy. Assigning 
farmers to training programs does not require knowing the exact cause of 
skill improvement — it only requires being able to rank which farmers are 
most likely to benefit. Even if other unmeasured factors influence the 
results, the relative ranking between farmers remains a useful guide for 
allocation decisions. A more rigorous test of whether training actually 
causes skill gains would require a controlled experiment, which is an 
important next step. For now, SAR provides a practical starting point 
that can be refined once field data from actual deployments become available.

\section{Methodology}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Data and Study Context}

The data come from a panel survey of farmers in Thailand's Smart Farmer 
program, collected between 2018 and 2020. The program covers smallholder 
farmers across five regions and measures five skill areas: production 
management, input management, technology adoption, analysis and planning, 
and marketing. Farmers were assessed before and after training using a 
validated 5-point scale administered by certified extension officers. 
The main outcome variable, $Ch\_Skill$, is the difference between the 
post-training and pre-training skill scores.

Missing values were rare (under 5\% for all variables). For binary and 
asset variables, missing values were replaced with zero, since not having 
an asset is itself meaningful information. For continuous skill variables, 
missing values were replaced with the column mean, which is appropriate 
given their roughly symmetric distributions. All numeric variables were 
then standardised to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. 
Replacing missing values with medians instead of means produced no 
meaningful difference in results.

\textit{Ethics and data access:}
This study followed the research protocols of the Department of Agricultural 
Extension, Ministry of Agriculture and Cooperatives, Thailand. All records 
were anonymised before analysis under a formal data-sharing agreement, 
and no personal information was used. Farmers gave informed consent when 
registering for the Smart Farmer program. Further details are provided 
in the supplementary materials.

\subsection{Farmer Segmentation}

K-Means clustering \citep{macqueen1967} was applied to 15 baseline variables 
covering skills, demographics, and risk perceptions. These include 
\textit{age, education, agricultural experience, irrigation access, loan 
access, average production management skill, average input management skill, 
average technology adoption skill, average analysis and planning skill, 
average marketing skill, average networking skill, production risk perception, 
input risk perception, market risk perception,} and \textit{financial risk 
perception}.

To decide how many clusters to use, we tested four measures of cluster 
quality \citep{rouseeuw1987}: the Elbow method, Silhouette score, 
Calinski--Harabasz (CH), and Davies--Bouldin (DB). The Elbow curve 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:elbow}) shows a clear bend at $k = 3$, giving Silhouette 
$= 0.185$, CH $= 202.67$, and DB $= 1.835$. The Silhouette score is modest, 
but this is expected for survey data measured on Likert scales, which tend 
to produce soft cluster boundaries \citep{rouseeuw1987}. All four measures 
pointed to $k = 3$ as the most sensible choice.

We ran two additional checks to confirm this. First, we tested $k = 2$ to 
$5$ and found that the main differences between segments stayed the same 
across all values (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}). Second, we compared K-Means 
against a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) with $k = 3$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:gmm}). 
The two methods agreed moderately (ARI $= 0.28$), but GMM produced uneven 
group sizes and a lower Silhouette score ($= 0.078$). K-Means gave more 
balanced and easier-to-interpret groups, so we kept it as our main approach.

Choosing $k = 3$ also fits naturally with Thailand's Smart Farmer program, 
which already uses three farmer tiers: Smart Farmer, Smart Farmer in 
Progress, and Knowledge Farmer. This makes the three segments directly 
useful for program planning.

\subsection{Predictive Modelling}

We used a Random Forest regression model \citep{breiman2001} to predict 
skill change ($Ch\_Skill$) from 18 variables covering farmer demographics, 
baseline skills, risk perceptions, and program participation. Because each 
farmer appears twice in the dataset — once before and once after training — 
we used GroupKFold cross-validation with 5 folds, grouping by farmer ID. 
This prevents the model from being tested on data from a farmer it has 
already seen during training. We also trained an XGBoost model 
\citep{chen2016} on the same variables as an independent check.

\subsection{Baseline Models and Performance Evaluation}
 
We compared the Random Forest against two simpler baselines: ordinary least 
squares (OLS) and ridge regression. All models were evaluated using $R^{2}$, 
RMSE, and MAE under 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation. Random Forest 
settings were chosen through a grid search over \texttt{n\_estimators} 
$\in$ \{300, 500\}, \texttt{max\_depth} $\in$ \{5, 8, None\}, 
\texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} $\in$ \{5, 10, 20\}, and \texttt{max\_features} 
$\in$ \{\texttt{sqrt}, 0.5\}. The best settings were: \texttt{n\_estimators} 
$= 500$, \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} $= 10$, and \texttt{max\_features} 
$=$ \texttt{sqrt}. As a robustness check, we also tested these fixed settings 
without re-tuning on each fold. This simpler approach gave $R^{2} = 0.268$ 
($\pm$ 0.039), very close to the tuned model, confirming that our results 
are not driven by overfitting during tuning. Table~\ref{tab:baseline_performance} 
reports the full results.

Within SAR, the Random Forest plays a structural role — it is used to 
generate SHAP values, not to make standalone predictions. As \citet{lundberg2020} 
show, SHAP values can be reliable even when overall model accuracy is 
moderate, as long as the model picks up real patterns in the data. The 
observed $R^{2} = 0.283$ suggests the Random Forest is capturing non-linear 
relationships that simple linear models miss.

\subsection{Explainable AI Analysis}

We used TreeExplainer \citep{lundberg2017} to calculate SHAP values, which 
show how much each variable contributed to each prediction. Overall feature 
importance was measured as the average absolute SHAP value across all farmers 
\citep{lundberg2020}. We then repeated this analysis separately within each 
farmer segment to identify which variables mattered most for each group — 
this is the \textit{Attribute} stage of SAR.

To check whether the results depended on the choice of model, we repeated 
the same SHAP analysis using XGBoost \citep{chen2016} and compared the 
feature rankings using Spearman rank correlation.

\subsection{Decision Support Framework}

SAR works in three stages (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}). First, in the 
\textit{Segment} stage, each farmer is assigned to one of three groups 
using K-Means clustering. Second, in the \textit{Attribute} stage, SHAP 
values are calculated within each group to identify which variables matter 
most for that segment. Third, in the \textit{Recommend} stage, these 
findings are converted into simple IF--THEN rules using a decision tree.

\subsection{Rule Extraction Method}

Rule extraction involves two steps.

In Step~1 (variable selection), we ranked variables within each segment 
by their average absolute SHAP value and selected the top four. These 
four variables together account for more than 61\% of the total SHAP 
contribution within each segment (range 60--62\%).

In Step~2 (threshold setting), we fitted a decision tree of depth 3 on 
these four variables to find the cut-off values used in the rules 
\citep{guidotti2019,molnar2022}.

The decision tree classifies farmers in the High-skill segment as having 
\textit{high training potential}, and farmers in the Low- and Moderate-skill 
segments as having \textit{low or moderate training potential}. The goal is 
to identify which farmers are most likely to benefit from advanced training.

This process does not involve circular reasoning. The clustering step uses 
only baseline variables that describe each farmer before training. The 
Random Forest and SHAP analysis use observed skill change ($Ch\_Skill$) 
as the outcome. The decision tree then approximates the SHAP-based 
decision boundary using a small set of variables — it does not use the 
cluster labels themselves.

We report accuracy on the full dataset ($n = 817$; 16 farmers were 
removed due to missing values in the selected variables). We also report 
cross-validated and held-out accuracy to check how well the rules 
work on new data.

To test generalisation, we split the data into 80\% for training and 
20\% for testing at the farmer level. The decision tree was trained only 
on the 80\% subset, using cluster assignments from that subset alone. 
Farmers in the test set were assigned to the nearest cluster centre from 
the training model. This ensures the test set contains no information 
used during training.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:rules} summarises the procedure.


\begin{algorithm}
\caption{SHAP-guided Surrogate Rule Extraction}
\label{alg:rules}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Cluster assignments $C$, SHAP matrix $S$, feature matrix $X$,
         tree depth $d = 3$, top-$K = 4$
\Ensure Set of IF--THEN rules $\mathcal{R}$
\State $\mathcal{R} \gets \emptyset$
\For{each cluster $c$ in $C$}
  \State $\phi_j \gets \text{mean}(|S_{c,j}|)$
         \Comment{Mean absolute SHAP value per feature}
  \State $\mathcal{F}_c \gets \text{Top-}4(\phi)$
         \Comment{Select features with highest SHAP magnitude}
  \State Fit surrogate tree $T_c$ on $X[\mathcal{F}_c]$
         to approximate SHAP-informed decision boundaries
  \State Extract rules $\mathcal{R}_c$ from leaf paths of $T_c$
  \State $\mathcal{R} \gets \mathcal{R} \cup \mathcal{R}_c$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathcal{R}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Clustering Robustness}

We tested cluster solutions from $k = 2$ to $k = 8$. The key differences 
between the Low-skill, Moderate-skill, and High-skill groups remained 
consistent across all values of $k$. This confirms that the three-segment 
structure is stable and not sensitive to the choice of $k$.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

We tested how much each part of SAR contributes to the final result by 
comparing the full system against two simpler versions.

The first version uses clustering only — it assigns farmers to groups 
based on their overall skill score (\texttt{All\_Skill}) and uses a 
simple threshold to recommend training, without any machine learning.
The second version uses Random Forest predictions only, without farmer 
segmentation, applying a single threshold across all farmers.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, the full SAR system achieves the 
highest accuracy (93.3\%), ahead of the clustering-only version (89.1\%) 
and the Random Forest-only version (79.3\%).

The clustering-only version works reasonably well because overall skill 
is a strong predictor on its own. However, it cannot detect interactions 
between variables and does not produce segment-specific rules. The 
Random Forest-only version misses the farmer grouping structure that 
makes the rules practical for policy use. Together, these results show 
that all three SAR components are needed to get the best performance.

\subsection{Farmer Segmentation}

K-Means with $k = 3$ produced three clearly different farmer groups, shown 
in Fig.~\ref{fig:clusters}. The quality of the clustering is supported by 
four measures: Silhouette $= 0.185$, CH $= 202.67$, and DB $= 1.835$ 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:elbow}). Table~\ref{tab:cluster_profile} shows the average 
profile of each group.

We confirmed the three-group structure using two additional checks 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}, Fig.~\ref{fig:gmm}). The main differences 
between groups stayed consistent when we tested $k = 2$ to $5$. Compared 
to K-Means, the GMM alternative (ARI $= 0.28$) produced less balanced 
groups that were harder to interpret, so we kept K-Means as our main 
approach.

The \textbf{Low-skill} group ($n = 271$) consists of older farmers (mean 
age 54.9 years) with the lowest education levels (mean 2.9) and the 
weakest starting skills. This group showed a small decline in average 
skill score ($Ch\_Skill = -0.233$), which is likely due to 
regression-to-the-mean in repeated assessments \citep{davis2012}. 
These farmers need foundational training that builds basic skills first.

The \textbf{Moderate-skill} group ($n = 228$) has mid-level skills and 
the highest concern about market risk (mean 3.23). Their skill score 
barely changed ($Ch\_Skill = -0.105$), suggesting that standard training 
programs do not work well for this group and that more targeted content 
is needed \citep{anderson2004}.

The \textbf{High-skill} group ($n = 334$) has the strongest starting 
skills, the highest participation rate in the Smart Farmer program 
(69.8\%), and the only positive average skill change ($Ch\_Skill = +0.259$). 
This fits the well-known pattern that farmers with stronger foundations 
tend to gain more from training \citep{feder1985,davis2012}.

\subsection{Global SHAP Importance and Attribution Robustness}

Fig.~\ref{fig:importance} shows the overall importance of each variable 
based on SHAP values. Overall skill level (\texttt{All\_Skill}) is by 
far the strongest predictor, followed by production management skill 
(\texttt{Avg\_ProdManage}), education (\texttt{edu}), marketing skill 
(\texttt{Avg\_Mkting}), and irrigation access (\texttt{irriga}).

The importance of \texttt{All\_Skill} fits with what we know about how 
people learn — farmers who start with stronger skills tend to gain more 
from training \citep{feder1985}. The secondary variables suggest that 
technical skills and education also play a role. Interestingly, the type 
of training program a farmer attended contributed very little, suggesting 
that what farmers bring to training matters more than the training itself 
\citep{ryo2022}.

To check whether these findings depend on the choice of model, we repeated 
the SHAP analysis using XGBoost. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}, 
the two models ranked variables very similarly ($\rho = 0.793$, 
$p < 0.001$), with three of the top four variables matching. This 
suggests the results reflect real patterns in the data, not just 
features of the Random Forest model \citep{lundberg2020}.

\subsection{SHAP Beeswarm Analysis}

Fig.~\ref{fig:shap} shows how each variable pushed predictions up or down 
for individual farmers. For \texttt{All\_Skill}, farmers with high starting 
skills received positive contributions while those with low starting skills 
received negative ones — a clear and consistent pattern. For 
\texttt{irriga}, most contributions were negative, suggesting that farmers 
with irrigation access may already have stronger skills to begin with, 
leaving less room for measured improvement. These patterns show that the 
model is picking up non-linear relationships that a simple linear model 
would miss.

\subsection{Cluster-Level SHAP Analysis}

Fig.~\ref{fig:clusterShap} shows SHAP values calculated separately for 
each farmer group. Overall skill (\texttt{All\_Skill}) is the strongest 
predictor in all three groups, but the second and third most important 
variables differ across groups.

In the \textbf{Low-skill} group, weak production management skills and 
low education pull predictions down the most. In the \textbf{Moderate-skill} 
group, no single variable stands out — contributions are spread fairly 
evenly across variables. In the \textbf{High-skill} group, marketing 
skill (\texttt{Avg\_Mkting}) becomes noticeably more important than in 
the other groups.

The SHAP rankings are more consistent across runs for the Low- and 
Moderate-skill groups than for the High-skill group, suggesting there 
is more uncertainty about which variables drive outcomes for advanced 
farmers. This confirms that looking at overall feature importance across 
all farmers is not enough — different groups need different training 
approaches.

\subsection{Decision Rule Extraction and Validation}

We fitted a decision tree of depth 3 on the top four SHAP-selected 
variables (Fig.~\ref{fig:rules}). The tree correctly classified 93.3\% 
of farmers in the full dataset ($n = 817$).

To check how well the rules work on new farmers, we held out 20\% of 
farmers for testing. The tree was trained on the remaining 80\% ($n = 320$), 
using cluster assignments from that subset only. Test farmers ($n = 81$) 
were assigned to the nearest cluster centre from the training model. 
The held-out accuracy was \textbf{91.4\%}, with similar precision and 
recall for both the Low/Moderate and High groups (F1 $\approx 0.91$ 
for both).

Five-fold cross-validation on the full dataset gave an average accuracy 
of \textbf{95.3\% $\pm$ 2.5\%}.

Table~\ref{tab:surr_validation} summarises all three results. The similar 
accuracy across all three tests shows that the rules work well on farmers 
not seen during training — they are not simply memorising the training data.

The decision tree produces three practical rules (simplified from the 
full depth-3 tree):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IF} Avg\_ProdManage $\leq$ 3.675 \textbf{THEN} recommend 
    foundational or remedial training (Low-skill pathway).
    \item \textbf{IF} Avg\_ProdManage $>$ 3.675 AND All\_Skill $\leq$ 4.0 
    \textbf{THEN} recommend intermediate training focusing on production 
    management and marketing.
    \item \textbf{IF} All\_Skill $>$ 4.0 \textbf{THEN} recommend advanced 
    training, including market-oriented and peer-mentoring modules 
    (High-skill pathway).
\end{itemize}

These rules match the three farmer tiers already used in Thailand's Smart 
Farmer program, making them easy for extension officers to apply in practice.

\section{Discussion}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Predictive Performance in Context}

The Random Forest achieved $R^{2} = 0.283$ under farmer-level 
cross-validation, slightly better than both linear and ridge regression 
($R^{2} = 0.257$). This improvement comes from the Random Forest's 
ability to detect non-linear patterns and interactions between variables 
that linear models cannot find. A modest $R^{2}$ is expected in studies 
of human learning, where skill change also depends on factors like 
motivation, engagement, and trainer quality that are hard to measure 
\citep{davis2012}.

Within SAR, the Random Forest is not meant to make precise predictions — 
its main role is to generate reliable SHAP values. As \citet{lundberg2020} 
show, SHAP values can still be accurate and useful even when overall model 
accuracy is moderate, as long as the model captures real patterns in the 
data. This is confirmed here by the strong agreement between SHAP rankings 
from the Random Forest and XGBoost ($\rho = 0.793$, Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}). 
In SAR, the final decisions come from the surrogate rules, not from the 
model's raw predictions — so what matters most is whether the SHAP values 
and rules are consistent, not whether every prediction is precise.

\subsection{Segment-Specific Drivers and Negative Skill Change}

The segment-level SHAP results show that the variables driving skill 
change are different across the three groups — a pattern that would be 
missed if we only looked at overall feature importance. In all three 
groups, starting skill level (\texttt{All\_Skill}) is the strongest 
driver, which fits with established research on how people learn 
\citep{feder1985,davis2012}.

The slight decline in average skill for the Low-skill ($-0.233$) and 
Moderate-skill ($-0.105$) groups has two likely explanations. First, 
farmers who scored lowest at the start are more likely to score higher 
on a second assessment just by chance — a well-known statistical pattern 
called regression-to-the-mean \citep{davis2012}. Second, Low-skill 
farmers had much lower participation in the Smart Farmer program (28.8\%) 
compared to High-skill farmers (69.8\%), meaning many in this group may 
have received little or no training at all \citep{anderson2004}.

In the High-skill group, marketing skill (\texttt{Avg\_Mkting}) stands 
out as more important than in the other groups. This suggests that for 
farmers who have already mastered the basics, the next barrier to 
improvement is market knowledge — pointing to a need for more advanced, 
market-focused training for this group.

\subsection{Decision Rule Validity and Generalisation}

The decision tree correctly classified 93.3\% of farmers in the full 
dataset. When tested on farmers not seen during training, accuracy 
dropped only slightly to 91.4\%, and 5-fold cross-validation gave 
95.3\% $\pm$ 2.5\%. The fact that all three tests gave similar results 
confirms that the rules work well on new farmers, not just the ones 
used to build them.

The two-step process — using SHAP to choose variables and a decision 
tree to find the cut-off values — means the rules are both grounded in 
the data and easy to explain \citep{molnar2022}. The cut-off values in 
the rules also match closely with the skill levels used in Thailand's 
Smart Farmer program tiers, which makes them practical to use within 
the existing system. Following \citet{rudin2019}, these rules should 
be seen as practical decision guides rather than an exact copy of what 
the Random Forest does internally.

\subsection{Operational Deployment Scenario}

SAR can be set up as a simple, rule-based tool that extension officers 
use without any machine learning knowledge. A typical workflow has 
four steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Record the farmer's background information (age, education, 
  skills, and resources)
  \item Assign the farmer to one of three groups using the K-Means model
  \item Apply the decision rules for that group
  \item Give the farmer a specific training recommendation
\end{enumerate}

This process can run through a simple interface — no direct access to 
the model is needed. Because the rules are written in plain IF--THEN 
format, anyone using the system can see exactly how each recommendation 
was made, which is important for public-sector programs that must be 
transparent and accountable.

Three key findings stand out from the analysis:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Starting skill level is the strongest predictor of learning 
  gains across all three groups.
  \item Different groups face different barriers: Low- and Moderate-skill 
  farmers are held back by weak production skills and low education, 
  while High-skill farmers are limited by their marketing knowledge.
  \item Offering the same training to all farmers does not work well, 
  as shown by the flat or negative skill change in the Low- and 
  Moderate-skill groups.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

This study has several limitations that should be kept in mind. First, 
all findings show associations between variables — they do not prove 
that any variable causes skill change. Establishing causality would 
require a controlled experiment or a quasi-experimental design. Second, 
the farmer segments and decision rule thresholds were built using data 
from Thailand's Smart Farmer program. Applying SAR in a different 
country or program would require rebuilding the segments and thresholds 
using local data. Third, the Silhouette scores are modest, meaning the 
three groups overlap somewhat — they should be treated as practical 
working categories rather than sharply distinct types. Fourth, while 
held-out testing confirms the rules generalise to new farmers, real-world 
testing in actual extension programs is still needed before full 
deployment. Finally, following \citet{rudin2019}, the decision tree rules 
are an approximation of the Random Forest — they capture the main 
patterns but do not reproduce every detail of the model.

%%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This study presents a decision support system for allocating agricultural 
training programs more effectively, tested on data from $n = 833$ farmers 
in Thailand's Smart Farmer initiative.

SAR combines farmer segmentation, predictive modelling, and explainable 
AI into a single pipeline that takes farmer data as input and produces 
clear training recommendations as output. The results show that starting 
skill level, production management ability, and education are the most 
important drivers of training outcomes — but their relative importance 
differs across farmer groups. Checking SHAP results against XGBoost 
($\rho = 0.793$) confirms that these findings reflect real patterns in 
the data, not just features of one particular model.

The main contribution of this study is showing how to turn segment-level 
SHAP results into simple IF--THEN rules that extension officers can use 
directly. These rules performed consistently across all three evaluation 
methods — in-sample (93.3\%), held-out (91.4\%), and cross-validated 
(95.3\% $\pm$ 2.5\%) — confirming they work well on farmers not seen 
during training.

Rather than aiming for the highest possible prediction accuracy, SAR 
is designed to be transparent, consistent, and practical to deploy. 
It offers a concrete step toward bringing explainable AI into real 
agricultural extension systems.

Future work should focus on three areas: (i) testing the rules in 
other agricultural programs and countries, (ii) using controlled 
experiments to measure whether the recommended training actually 
improves farmer skills, and (iii) connecting SAR to live data systems 
so recommendations can be updated as new data come in 
\citep{yang2025,zhang2026}.
