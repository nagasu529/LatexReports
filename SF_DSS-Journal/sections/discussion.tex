\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Predictive Performance in Context}

The Random Forest model explains 13\% of the variance in skill improvement, a level of explanatory power that is typical for observational studies of human learning outcomes. While linear regression achieves a higher $R^2$, its additive structure limits its ability to capture the non-linear threshold effects and interaction patterns revealed by SHAP. The value of the Random Forest in this study therefore lies not in predictive superiority, but in its capacity to uncover structural relationships that support explainability and rule extraction. This distinction is important for agricultural extension systems, where transparent decision logic is often more valuable than marginal gains in predictive accuracy.

\subsection{Segment-Specific Insights}

The integration of clustering and SHAP analysis reveals clear heterogeneity in learning mechanisms across farmer segments. Low-skill farmers benefit primarily from foundational competencies, suggesting that basic capability strengthening remains a prerequisite for effective training uptake. Moderate-skill farmers show strong sensitivity to marketing and production management skills, indicating that targeted reinforcement in these domains may yield substantial improvements. High-skill farmers exhibit attenuated or negative SHAP contributions, consistent with saturation effects and limited room for measurable improvement. These findings align with established theories of heterogeneous learning pathways in agricultural extension and highlight the importance of segment-aware training design.

\subsection{Decision Rule Validity}

The surrogate decision tree provides a transparent approximation of the Random Forest’s decision structure, translating complex model behavior into interpretable IF–THEN rules. These rules reflect the dominant SHAP-derived features—marketing capability, managerial readiness, and risk attitudes—and offer a practical mechanism for guiding training allocation. Because the surrogate model is derived from SHAP-selected features rather than raw predictors, the resulting rules are both theoretically grounded and empirically interpretable. This approach demonstrates how explainable AI can bridge the gap between predictive modeling and operational decision-making in agricultural extension.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the dataset is observational and non-experimental, meaning that all findings reflect associative rather than causal relationships. Second, the modest $R^2$ values indicate that unobserved behavioral and contextual factors likely influence training outcomes. Third, the clustering structure is based on baseline features and may evolve as farmers acquire new competencies. Future work should validate the framework in prospective field trials, incorporate longitudinal behavioral indicators, and explore alternative segmentation methods such as latent class analysis. Extending the framework to other agricultural training contexts would further assess its generalizability and policy relevance.
